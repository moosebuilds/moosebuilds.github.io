Moose Builds it does not pulse

Two pulsing dots were eating half my GPU

5 AUGUST 2026

Theo put out a video yesterday about T3 Code hammering his GPU on a page that wasn't doing anything. Infinite CSS animations on little status icons. I had the same bug in June. It cost me two days.

What got me writing it up is the comment section. Plenty of people recognised the symptom, nobody explained the mechanism. Someone asked outright and got no reply. So here it is, including the half I don't think either of us worked out on our own.

What it looked like

Mine showed up as dwm.exe, the Windows compositor, sat at 50 to 70 percent of the GPU while nothing on screen was moving. Every browser process reading zero. Task Manager pointing at nobody.

His was the browser's own GPU process eating half a core on a 5K display, with the tab itself claiming 1 to 4 percent.

Either way, the number you'd naturally go and check is the one that tells you nothing.

DevTools won't help you here, and it's worth knowing why before you waste an evening in there like I did. They're built for JavaScript that shouldn't be running, slow loads, resources that don't turn up. Once the work has been handed off to the compositor there's almost nothing left on the main thread for them to see. Having them open changes the measurement too. You want something outside the browser entirely.

Finding the tab

Theo's method was blunt and it worked. Open a browser you never use, put one tab in it, compare. Google's homepage at 3 percent, his app at 18 and climbing. Thirty seconds, no tooling, and if you're on a machine you don't control it's the right first move.

Mine was fussier because Windows hands you better instruments. Per-process, per-engine GPU load is just a performance counter:

Get-Counter "\GPU Engine(*)\Utilization Percentage"

That tells you the compositor is burning. It doesn't tell you who's making it burn, because the compositor sits downstream of everybody. So I suspended each browser renderer in turn and watched the number. One of them took it from 51 percent to 4.7. That was my terminal tab, and the browser's own task manager agreed, naming the same process.

PresentMon gives you the frame-level view if you want it. Anything presenting in Composed: something is forcing the desktop to recomposite, anything in Hardware: Independent Flip bypasses it and costs nothing. One thing that cost me an hour: Chromium pushes every tab's compositing through a single GPU process, so PresentMon can name the browser and never the tab.

Two separate things

The first is which property you animate. Touch box-shadow, or filter, or anything else that affects painting, and you get a full repaint of that element every frame. transform and opacity don't do that, they're handled on the compositor. Old advice, and it's right.

That was my first fix. The attention pulse on my status dots was animating an expanding box-shadow. I moved it to a transform-and-opacity ring on a pseudo-element. Looks identical, costs a fraction. Idle load went from the mid 50s into the teens.

Then it still spiked into the 80s whenever a few dots lit at once, which is the second thing.

An animation that never ends promotes its element to its own compositor layer and then holds the compositor committing frames at your display's refresh rate. Forever. Whether anything visibly changed or not. The page never gets to go idle. Doesn't matter that opacity is the cheap one.

It scales with your monitor, which is why some people hit this and some never will. Theo was on 5K at 120Hz. I'm on an ultrawide at 240, about 16 million pixels, so mine was doing roughly twice the work per second. On a 1080p 60Hz panel the same code is probably fine forever.

Tailwind's animate-pulse is an infinite opacity animation, which is why it turns up in both stories. Nothing wrong with the class. It just has no idea you're about to put fourteen of them on one screen.

will-change makes it worse

Counterintuitive, this one. I saw somebody in Theo's comments recommend the opposite in good faith, off real experience.

will-change: opacity does exactly what it says. Promotes the element to its own layer and keeps it there. If you're fixing a janky one-off transition, that's a genuine fix, because you've paid the layer cost up front instead of in the middle of the animation. If it's a permanent indicator, you've just made the layer permanent as well.

I had fourteen status dots, every one with the hint on it. Fourteen pinned layers that were never allowed to go away. Taking it off was worth more than all the animation tuning put together.

Rule of thumb. will-change is for transient stuff. Never put it on anything that sits on screen all the time.

What fixed it

Made the pulse finite.

/* before */
animation: glow-breathe 1.4s steps(12, end) infinite;
will-change: opacity;

/* after */
opacity: 0;
animation: glow-breathe 1.4s steps(12, end) 3;

Three pulses when the state changes, then it settles to a solid dot. You still get the flick of movement that catches your eye at the moment something happens. You don't get a page that never stops rendering. Idle went under 12 percent and stayed there.

The indicators that stay lit for a long stretch, the microphone glow while it's recording, I just made static. A steady glow says "recording" perfectly well, and animating it was costing about 70 percent of the compositor for the entire length of the recording.

Codex suggested the finite pulse to Theo, more or less word for word, and he called it the worst guidance he'd ever heard. I get the reflex. It arrives sounding like a compromise. It isn't one, and it looks fine.

Don't trust an isolated test page

I built one. Four animation variants, measured each on the real monitor, picked the cheapest. It lied to me.

A throttled opacity pulse at about 12 frames a second measured beautifully on its own, then did nothing at all on the real page. On its own it was the only thing animating, so the page still dropped out of composited mode between frames. On the real page, surrounded by other permanent animations, it never got the chance.

The mode switch belongs to the whole page, not to the one animation you're testing. Theo's approach was better and I'd use it next time. He got an agent to build him a console harness he could paste straight into the live page, flipping whole categories of CSS on and off while he watched the number move.

The fix isn't the deliverable

This is the bit I'd actually argue about.

Nothing in a codebase stops the next person adding a pulsing icon, because the code that does it looks completely innocent. Theo says as much on camera about another app. Fixed, then broken again by the next update, over and over.

So I shipped three things rather than one.

  1. A probe that samples the compositor and ranks every process forcing a recomposite, tracing each one back to the application that owns it.
  2. A before-and-after gate. Save a baseline, make the change, run it again. It fails if the compositor jumped, or if a new composited presenter turned up that wasn't there before. It runs on anything that touches the interface.
  3. An overlay on a keyboard shortcut that walks document.getAnimations(), lists what's running, and marks the infinite ones in red.

That last one took twenty minutes and it's the one I'd build first if I had the two days again. The whole trouble with this bug is that the code reads fine, the page looks fine, and the cost turns up three layers away in a process you'd never think to open.
